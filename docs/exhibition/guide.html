Part A · Know the project

1. The pitch (learn this by heart)

30-second pitch "Map apps find the fastest route. But for a wheelchair user, an elderly person, a pregnant woman or a parent with a stroller, the fastest route often has stairs, a broken lift or a packed bus, and they only find out at the station. TransitSwap plans metro, bus, auto and walking journeys in Bengaluru and checks every route against the rider's needs before ranking it. A route the rider cannot use is never recommended, and the app explains why each route was removed. It also learns each rider's preferences from their choices. In our tests on 520 simulated trips, a normal fastest-route planner gave wheelchair users an unusable route on 35.6 % of trips. TransitSwap: zero."
10-second version "It's a Bengaluru journey planner that never recommends a route you physically can't use, and tells you why."

The one big idea

Constraints first, ranking second. First remove every route the rider cannot use (hard rules). Then rank only the routes that are left (preferences). Normal planners do it the other way round, or only give accessibility a "bonus point", so an unusable route can still win.

Easy analogy: a waiter serving a customer with a peanut allergy does not just "rank peanut dishes lower". He removes them from the menu, then recommends the best of what is left. TransitSwap does the same with stairs, lifts and crowds.

2. The problem

  • Normal navigation apps optimise one thing, usually travel time.
  • Riders with mobility needs care about other things first: is there a lift, how many stairs, how far is the walk, is the bus too crowded to board?
  • When an app gives them an unusable route, they discover it only on arrival: a staircase with no lift, a crowded platform. The trip fails.
  • Even apps with a "wheelchair" option usually have one switch for everyone. A senior, a pregnant rider and a parent with a stroller have different limits.
  • Apps rarely explain why a route was chosen or removed, so riders cannot trust them.

Background fact you may quote: India's 2011 Census counted about 2.68 crore people with disabilities (about 2.2 % of the population), and the elderly population is growing. UN Sustainable Development Goal 11.2 asks for accessible transport for all, "with special attention to" persons with disabilities and older persons.

3. The solution: what TransitSwap does

Plans multimodal journeys

Combines Namma Metro, BMTC buses, autos and walking in one trip, with fare, time, walking distance and transfers for each option.

Seven travel profiles

Standard, wheelchair, senior, pregnant, stroller, heavy luggage and reduced mobility. Each profile has its own rules.

Removes unusable routes, with reasons

Every removed route gets a typed reason: station (not accessible, stairs, no lift), walking distance, or crowd. Shown as "N other routes were excluded, see why".

Ranks what is left

Scores the remaining routes on 9 factors: time, cost, walking, reliability, accessibility, crowd, weather, missed-connection risk and transfers.

Learns you (TransitDNA)

Learns which factors you care about from the routes you pick. After 5 choices it trains a personal model. You can see and reset your weights.

Tells you how sure it is

Arrival time range (90 % / 95 %), a 0 to 100 reliability score, and the chance of missing a connection (Monte Carlo simulation, 1,000 trials).

Real conditions

Live weather forecast for the hour you travel (Open-Meteo), crowd estimates by time of day, walking paths along real streets (OpenStreetMap).

Live navigation

Turn-by-turn guidance with GPS and voice, reroutes when you go off route, and a simulation mode for demos. Bus and metro positions are not live-tracked (honest label).

Explains itself

"Why recommended" tags that are checked to be true against the other routes, plus a step-by-step journey with bus numbers, direction and next departures.

Full product around it

Sign-in, dashboard, history with ratings, saved routes, profile, a Research page with the benchmark, and an admin console for lifts, stations and crowd reports.

4. How it works (the pipeline)

Every time a rider presses Find Routes, the engine runs these six steps:

1Find stopsNearest metro stations and bus stops to the start and the destination.
2SearchGraph search joining walk, metro, bus and auto: at most 6 legs and 3 transfers.
3Keep good optionsPareto filter: drop a route if another is better or equal in time, fare, walking and transfers.
4Apply rulesHard accessibility rules for the profile. Unusable routes are removed with a typed reason.
5RankScore the survivors on 9 factors, with profile or learned (TransitDNA) weights.
6ExplainWhy-recommended tags, exclusion reasons, arrival range, connection risk.

Step 4 (red) is the heart of the project: it happens before ranking, so no score can bring an unusable route back.

The three kinds of "intelligence", said honestly

PartTypeWhat it does
TransitDNAMachine learningPairwise logistic regression. Learns how much a rider values time vs cost vs walking etc. from "chose route A over route B" examples.
Monte Carlo risk, arrival rangeStatisticsMonte Carlo replays the journey 1,000 times with real past delays to estimate the chance of missing a transfer. The arrival range uses the mean and standard deviation of past prediction errors.
Accessibility, crowd, reliabilityRulesClear, explainable rules (stairs, lifts, walking caps, crowd level). Rules are the right tool here: safety limits should not be "learned".
Never say "the whole thing is AI" or "deep learning". Say: "One part learns (TransitDNA); the safety part is rules on purpose; the uncertainty part is statistics." Judges respect this.

Two extra hard rules

  • Walking cap: wheelchair, stroller and reduced-mobility routes with more than 1.2 km of total walking or rolling are removed.
  • Crowd rule: for wheelchair, senior, pregnant, stroller and luggage, a route through a station with a HIGH crowd at that time is removed.   • Missing data never blocks a route; it shows a warning instead ("open-world" policy).
Be ready: the limits (150 m, 1.2 km, 15 / 20 / 35 stairs) are design assumptions, not medical standards. Say: "They are configurable, and in a real deployment they should be set with disability organisations and, ideally, by each rider."

9. Limitations and future work

Limitations (say them before they are asked)

  • Synthetic data; one corridor of Bengaluru, not the whole city
  • No study with real riders yet
  • No live bus or metro positions, no live lift-outage feed
  • Learned model does not beat the rules on the synthetic test
  • Thresholds are assumptions, not clinically validated
  • Not yet tested on real phones, Safari/Firefox, or with screen readers
  • Free hosting sleeps: first request can take about 30 seconds

Future work

  • User study with wheelchair users, older adults and caregivers
  • Real GTFS feeds and surveyed station accessibility data
  • Live lift status and crowd data from the operator or sensors
  • Thresholds set with disability organisations, per rider
  • Kannada and Hindi, screen-reader testing, a mobile app
  • A faster public-transit algorithm (e.g. RAPTOR) for the full city
  • More cities; offer the accessibility check as an API to other apps

Part C · Answer the judges

12. Likely questions with short answers

Answer in two or three sentences, then offer to show it. Red numbers mark the tough questions: practise those out loud.

A. The idea

1What is your project, in one line?

A journey planner for Bengaluru that combines metro, bus, auto and walking, and never recommends a route that a rider with a mobility need cannot actually use, and it explains why.

2What problem does it solve?

Map apps choose the fastest route. For a wheelchair user, senior, pregnant rider or parent with a stroller, that route may have stairs, no lift or a packed bus, and they find out only at the station. We check those things first.

3Who is it for?

Everyone, but especially seven kinds of riders: standard, wheelchair, senior, pregnant, stroller, heavy luggage and reduced mobility. Each has its own rules.

4Google Maps already exists. How are you different?

Google Maps is mainly about the fastest route and, in some cities, a single "wheelchair accessible" switch. We have seven profiles with different rules, remove unusable routes before ranking and say why each was removed, learn each rider's preferences, and show how reliable the arrival time is. Google has far better data; our contribution is the method, which could sit on top of real data.

5What is new or innovative here?

Three things: (1) constraints are applied before ranking, so no score can bring back an unusable route; (2) every removed route has a typed, readable reason; (3) a reproducible test that measures how often simpler planners give unusable routes (35.6 % for wheelchair users).

6Why Bengaluru?

It's our city, it has a growing metro with mixed accessibility, a large bus network and autos, so it is a realistic multimodal test case. We used real station names.

7Why the name "TransitSwap"?

A journey here swaps between modes (walk, metro, bus, auto), and the app swaps an unusable route for one you can actually take.

This is a suggested answer; use your team's own reason if you had one.
8Is it a mobile app or a website?

A responsive web app. It works in the browser on a phone or laptop with nothing to install. It can be turned into an installable app later.

9Is it actually working and deployed?

Yes. It is live at transit-swap.vercel.app, with the backend on Render and the database on MongoDB Atlas. You can try it now.

10What motivated you?

Watching elderly people and people with disabilities struggle with stairs, broken lifts and crowded buses. Apps tell you the fastest way, not a way you can actually use.

Add a real personal story if a team member has one: it lands well.

B. Accessibility

11What makes a route "accessible" in your system?

Every station on it has step-free access for that profile (lift or ramp, not too many stairs), the walking is short enough, the transport type suits the profile (for example no buses for wheelchair), and no station is very crowded at that time.

12What is the difference between a hard and a soft constraint?

Hard: a rule, the route is removed. Soft: a penalty, the route just scores lower and can still win. Safety needs hard rules; taste can be soft.

13Why not just give accessibility a very high weight in the score?

Because a weight can be outvoted: a much faster route can still win. And if no accessible route exists, a ranker still shows its "best" one, which is unusable. Our test shows it: soft scoring still gave wheelchair users an unusable route on 19.2 % of trips; hard rules give 0 %.

14What happens if no accessible route exists?

We say so honestly and list the routes that existed and why each was ruled out. The rider can change the time (crowds change) or take an auto or cab. We never fake a route.

15Why are buses avoided for wheelchair users?

Our data can't confirm that a bus is low-floor or has a ramp, and boarding a regular bus in a wheelchair is usually not possible. So we use metro plus autos or accessible cabs.

16How do you know which stations are accessible?

Each station has a record: lift, ramp, escalator, number of stairs, step-free platform, tactile paving, accessible toilet. In this prototype the records are synthetic. In real use they'd come from the metro operator, surveys and community reports, which the admin console already supports.

17What if information about a station is missing?

Missing never blocks a route; it shows a warning ("open-world" policy). Otherwise most routes would vanish because real data is incomplete. The trade-off: a station with a wrongly missing record could be allowed, so we warn the rider.

18What if a lift breaks down?

An admin marks it out of service and the very next search avoids that station. Our test: one lift outage at Majestic cut off 66 of 420 wheelchair trips and rerouted 56 more, so the system reacts correctly.

19How does the crowd rule work?

If a station is estimated HIGH crowd at your travel time, routes through it are removed for wheelchair, senior, pregnant, stroller and luggage riders. A reading is used only within 2 hours of your time on the same kind of day; otherwise the crowd is "unavailable" and doesn't block.

20Who decided 1.2 km, 150 m and the stair counts?

We did, as design assumptions, and we say so in the report. They are configurable. In a real deployment they should be set with disability organisations and, ideally, by each rider.

21Why seven separate profiles and not one "accessible" switch?

Needs are different. A wheelchair user needs a lift; a pregnant rider can climb stairs but should avoid packed buses; heavy luggage means an auto is best. One switch is either too strict or too loose.

22What about visually or hearing-impaired users?

They aren't separate routing profiles yet. The app supports keyboard-only use and voice guidance, and passes automated accessibility checks, but we haven't tested with screen-reader users. That is future work.

23Is your own app accessible?

Yes, as far as automated testing goes: skip links, full keyboard use, labelled controls. An axe-core scan of 87 screens at phone, tablet and laptop widths found 0 problems, and Lighthouse accessibility is 100. Not yet tested with real screen-reader users.

24Can a rider change profile any time?

Yes. Pick a profile on Plan Trip for that search, or set a default in Profile. The rules change instantly.

C. Algorithms and AI

25How does the app find routes?

Find the nearest stations, search the network for combinations of walk, metro, bus and auto (at most 6 legs and 3 transfers), keep only the good options (Pareto filter), remove the ones the rider cannot use, rank the rest on 9 factors, and explain the result.

26Which algorithm do you use? Dijkstra? A*?

A bounded multi-criteria graph search. Dijkstra and A* optimise one cost (like time); we have several (time, fare, walking, transfers), so we keep a set of best trade-offs. The limits (6 legs, 3 transfers) guarantee it finishes quickly. It isn't RAPTOR, the algorithm big apps use; that would be the upgrade for a full city.

27What is a Pareto filter?

We drop a route if another one is as good or better in every way (time, fare, walking, transfers). What is left are honest trade-offs: maybe one is faster and another cheaper.

28How are routes ranked?

Each route gets 9 scores between 0 and 1: time, cost, walking, reliability, accessibility, crowd, weather, missed-connection risk and transfers. They are combined with weights into one score out of 100. The weights come from the profile, or from your learned TransitDNA.

29Where exactly is the AI or machine learning?

In TransitDNA: a pairwise logistic regression that learns your personal weights from your route choices. The safety part is rules on purpose, and the uncertainty part is statistics (Monte Carlo and prediction intervals). We label each one honestly; we don't claim deep learning.

30How does TransitDNA learn?

Each time you choose route A when B was also offered, that's one example: "A beat B". The model learns which differences (faster, cheaper, less walking...) explain your choices. After 5 choices it trains your personal weights. You can see them on your profile and reset them.

31Explain logistic regression simply.

It takes the differences between two routes, multiplies each by a weight, adds them up, and turns the total into a probability between 0 and 1: "how likely you prefer A over B". Training adjusts the weights until those probabilities match your real choices.

32Why not deep learning or a neural network?

One rider gives tens of choices, not millions. A neural network would overfit and be a black box. Logistic regression learns from little data, is fast, and its weights are readable ("you care about walking twice as much as cost").

33How accurate is your model?

87.5 % on held-out test choices, against 91.7 % for the simple rule baseline. So on our synthetic test it does not beat the rules, and we report that. The synthetic choices were generated by rules, so rules naturally win there; the model's value is personalisation for real riders, which needs a real user study to measure.

34What about a new user with no history (cold start)?

They get sensible starting weights (Speed 25, Reliability 25, Walking 20, Cost 15, Access 15) or the preset for their profile. Learning starts after 5 choices.

35What is the Monte Carlo simulation?

For a route with a transfer we replay the journey 1,000 times, each time picking real past delays at random, and count how often the delay is longer than the transfer buffer. That count becomes the "chance of missing your connection".

36How do you give an arrival time range?

From past prediction errors: the average error and how spread out it is (standard deviation). 90 % range = average ± 1.645 × spread. With fewer than 5 past observations we show it as a rough estimate, not a confidence level.

37What is the reliability score?

A simple rule-based score from 0 to 100. It starts at 90 and goes up or down for transfers, bus vs metro, bad weather and crowds. It is labelled as an estimate, not a trained model.

38How is weather used?

We fetch the Open-Meteo forecast for the hour you travel. Rain or heat increases the walking penalty and shows a warning, so a route with less walking can move up.

39How do you explain the recommendation?

"Why recommended" tags like "least walking" are only shown if they are true compared with the other routes. Removed routes show the exact reason, such as "Trinity Metro is marked not accessible".

40How do you draw walking paths on real streets?

We ask OSRM, an open routing engine on OpenStreetMap, for the walking path and cache it for 6 hours. If the free server is busy, we show a straight line labelled "Approximate path", never a fake street path.

41How does live navigation work?

It follows your phone's GPS, speaks each instruction with the browser's speech feature, and when you go off route it plans again from your current position. A simulation mode replays the trip for demos. Bus and metro positions are not live-tracked, and the screen says so.

42How fast is it?

Checking one route against the rules takes under 0.3 ms. A whole search takes a few seconds, mostly waiting for the free street-routing server; repeats are instant because of caching.

D. Data

43Is your data real?

The station names are real; coordinates are approximate. Accessibility, crowd, fare and timetable data are synthetic: no station was surveyed. We say this in the app, the README, the report and the paper. Weather, maps, place search and walking paths are real.

44Why didn't you use real data?

There is no open, complete, surveyed accessibility dataset for Bengaluru stations, and real accessibility needs a physical survey that's beyond a student project. We designed the engine so real timetables (GTFS) and surveyed data plug in without changing the code.

45How big is your network?

3 metro lines with 41 stations, 10 bus routes with 24 stops, 65 accessibility records and 16 crowd readings. It covers one corridor; trips outside it get an "outside the demo network" message.

46How would you get real data?

Metro and bus timetables from the operators in GTFS format; station surveys with NGOs or volunteers; operator lift status; and community reports from riders, which our admin console can already moderate.

47Where does crowd information come from?

Synthetic station readings by time of day, plus reports riders submit in the app (used for 90 minutes, only for trips happening now). In future: ticket gate counts or sensors.

48What do you store about users?

Name, email, a hashed (unreadable) password, profile and preferences, learned weights, and the journeys, ratings and choices they save. Passwords are never stored in plain text.

E. Results and evaluation

49What is your main result?

On 520 simulated trips, a fastest-route planner gave wheelchair users an unusable route on 35.6 % of trips; soft scoring 19.2 %; TransitSwap 0 % for every profile. The cost: a median 5.7 minutes extra, only for the 17.4 % of trips that need a detour.

50How did you test that?

We took 520 start-to-destination pairs across the network, planned each for all 7 profiles with three planners (fastest, soft scoring, ours), and checked every recommended route against the profile's rules. The test is a script anyone can rerun and get the same numbers.

51What does "95 % CI 31.6 to 39.8 %" mean?

The true rate is very likely between 31.6 % and 39.8 %; 35.6 % is our best estimate from 520 trips. It shows the result isn't luck.

52Doesn't removing routes make journeys much longer?

Usually not. Most trips need no change at all; for the 17.4 % that do, the median extra time is 5.7 minutes. A slightly longer route you can use is better than a fast one you can't.

53What does "19.2 % have no step-free route" mean?

For about one wheelchair trip in five, there is no step-free route at all in the (synthetic) network. A normal planner hides that by showing an unusable route; we say it clearly, which is also useful to planners and the city.

54Did you test with real users?

Not yet, and we say so. A study with wheelchair users, older adults and caregivers is the first next step. What we did measure: correctness of the rules, the simulated benchmark, automated accessibility audits, and many automated tests.

55How do you compare with other planners?

The Research page compares our top route with 5 simpler planners (fastest, cheapest, least walking, fewest transfers, random). For a standard rider we agree with the fastest route 95 % of the time, as expected; the big differences appear for accessibility profiles.

56What is "1,680 route checks, 0 violations" on the home page?

An automated check: 7 profiles × 12 trips × 5 times of day × 4 kinds of weather = 1,680 searches. Every route TransitSwap recommended was checked against that profile's rules, and none broke a rule.

F. Technology and engineering

57What is your tech stack?

Frontend: React 19, TypeScript, Vite, Tailwind CSS and Leaflet maps. Backend: Node.js, Express, TypeScript. Database: MongoDB Atlas with Mongoose. Hosting: Vercel and Render. Tests: Vitest, Playwright, GitHub Actions.

58Why this stack?

One language (TypeScript) for the whole system, type safety catches mistakes early, it's widely used in industry, and it has free hosting, which matters for a student project.

59Explain the architecture.

Three tiers: the React app in the browser calls a REST API on the Express server; the server runs the engine and reads and writes MongoDB; it also calls free services for weather, places and street paths.

60Why MongoDB and not MySQL?

Our records, such as journeys with legs and station accessibility attributes, are nested documents, which fit MongoDB naturally. Atlas also gives a free cloud database.

61How is it secured?

Passwords hashed with bcrypt, JWT tokens for sign-in, helmet security headers, rate limiting (150 requests per 15 minutes), validation of every input, users see only their own data, and admin accounts can only be created on the server.

62How did you test the software?

360 frontend tests, 34 backend test suites (unit tests plus API tests on an in-memory database) and 7 end-to-end tests that drive the real app in a browser, including a keyboard-only journey. GitHub Actions runs them on every push.

63Why is it slow the first time?

The free Render server sleeps when idle and takes about 30 seconds to wake. The site sends a wake-up request as soon as it opens. A paid server wouldn't sleep.

64What if an outside service fails?

The app degrades gracefully: demo data is used and labelled, walking legs become "Approximate path", and the demo trips work without place search. It never crashes or pretends.

65Can it scale to the whole city or more users?

The API is stateless, so more servers can be added behind a load balancer, and results are cached. For the full city we'd load real GTFS, use a faster transit algorithm like RAPTOR, and run our own routing server instead of free public ones.

66Does it work offline?

No, it needs internet for planning, maps and weather. Offline saved routes could be added later.

67Does it work on phones?

Yes. The design is responsive and was checked automatically at phone, tablet and laptop sizes. It hasn't been tested on many physical phones yet.

68What does the admin console do?

Operators can mark lifts broken or fixed, edit station accessibility, and approve or reject crowd and accessibility reports. Changes take effect on the next search.

69How much does it cost to run?

Nothing now: all on free tiers. A real service would need a paid server that doesn't sleep, a paid database tier and its own routing server, roughly a few thousand rupees a month to start.

G. Impact, privacy and future

70What is the social impact?

People with disabilities, seniors and caregivers can trust the route before leaving home. That means fewer failed trips and more independence. It supports UN SDG 11.2, accessible transport for all.

71Who could use or adopt this?

Riders directly; metro and bus operators, to show accessible routes and find stations that most need lifts; NGOs; and other map apps, through the accessibility check as an API.

72Is there a business model?

Free for riders. Possible paths: partnership with transit operators or smart-city programmes, CSR funding, or licensing the accessibility engine to other apps.

73What about privacy of location?

Location is used in the browser for navigation and sent to the server only to plan or reroute. Journeys are stored only when you save them, passwords are hashed, data isn't shared or sold, and learned weights can be reset.

74What is next for the project?

A user study with real riders, real GTFS and surveyed station data, live lift and crowd feeds, thresholds set with disability groups, Kannada and Hindi, screen-reader testing, and a mobile app.

75What challenges did you face?
  • Free street-routing servers limiting requests: solved with caching, retries and honest "approximate" labels.
  • A crowd-data bug that blocked wheelchair routes at night: fixed with the 2-hour, same-day-type rule and tests.
  • Keeping the ML honest: a proper held-out test showed it doesn't beat the rules, and we report that.
  • Making our own app accessible: 87-screen audit, keyboard support, skip links.
76What did you learn?

Full-stack development and deployment, graph search and multi-criteria ranking, simple ML done properly with a held-out test, statistics for uncertainty, accessibility standards, and most of all being honest about what is measured and what isn't.

H. The tough ones (practise these)

78Isn't this just a filter? What is so special?

Yes, the core is a filter, and that is the point: safety should be a simple rule, not a hidden score. What's new is where it sits (before ranking), that every removal explains itself, how missing data is handled, and that we measured how often normal planners fail (35.6 %). Simple and proven beats complex and unproven.

79Your ML is worse than plain rules. Why keep it?

On synthetic data generated by rules, rules will always win. The model is there to personalise for real people whose preferences don't follow our rules. We kept the honest number instead of tuning the test to look good, and the safety rules never depend on the model.

80Synthetic data means your results mean nothing?

The exact percentages will change with real data, but the conclusion won't: a ranker can still recommend an unusable route, and a hard filter can't, by design. The synthetic network shows how big the difference can be and gives a test we can rerun on real data.

81If a lift breaks while I'm travelling, does the app know?

Only if an operator or rider reports it; we don't have a live feed from the metro yet. Once it is marked, the next search or reroute avoids that station. A live operator feed is planned future work.

82Why can't I see where my bus is right now?

Live bus and metro positions need the operators' real-time feeds, which we don't have. We use a demonstration timetable and the screen says clearly that vehicles aren't live-tracked.

83What if the rules are too strict and remove everything?

Then the app says no route fits and shows why. The rider can change the time or profile. Being told "no safe route" is better than being sent to a staircase. The limits are configurable.

84What did you build yourselves, and what did you use?

We built the routing engine, the accessibility rules, the ranking and learning, the statistics, the evaluation, the backend API and the whole interface. We used libraries for the UI framework, maps, database access and testing, and free services for weather, places and street paths.

85Did you use AI tools to write the code?

Answer honestly. For example: "We used AI coding assistants as a tool, as most developers do now, but the design decisions, the testing and the evaluation are ours, and we can explain any part of the code." Then offer to walk through a part.

86Why should a judge pick this project?

It solves a real problem for people often left out by navigation apps, it is fully working and deployed, it is backed by a measured result (35.6 % to 0 %), and it is honest about its limits.

13. One-page cheat sheet

What

Bengaluru journey planner (metro, bus, auto, walk) that never recommends a route you can't use, and explains why.

Big idea

Constraints first, ranking second. Hard rules remove unusable routes before any scoring.

For whom

7 profiles: standard, wheelchair, senior, pregnant, stroller, heavy luggage, reduced mobility.

How

Find stops → graph search (6 legs, 3 transfers) → Pareto filter → hard rules → rank on 9 factors → explain.

AI, honestly

TransitDNA = pairwise logistic regression (learns your weights after 5 choices). Rules for safety. Statistics for uncertainty (Monte Carlo 1,000 trials, 90/95 % arrival range).

Result

Unusable routes for wheelchair users: fastest 35.6 % · soft scoring 19.2 % · TransitSwap 0 %. Cost: 5.7 min median, for 17.4 % of trips.

Stack

React 19 + TypeScript + Tailwind + Leaflet · Node/Express · MongoDB Atlas · Vercel + Render · OSM, OSRM, Open-Meteo.

Honest limits

Synthetic data, simulated benchmark, no user study yet, no live vehicle tracking, ML 87.5 % vs rules 91.7 %.

Golden rules for answering

  1. Short first, detail if asked. Two or three sentences, then stop. Let the judge pull more.
  2. Show, don't tell. "Let me show you" and press the button. The Wheelchair demo is your strongest moment.
  3. Never overclaim. Say "synthetic data", "simulated benchmark", "no user study yet" before a judge says it. Honesty scores points.
  4. If you don't know: "We haven't measured that yet. Here's how we would." Never guess a number.
  5. Use the analogy: the allergy menu: remove what is unsafe, then recommend the best of the rest.
  6. One voice at a time. Whoever owns the topic answers; others add only if needed. Look at the judge and thank them for each question.
